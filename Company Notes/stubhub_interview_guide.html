📞 Recruiter Call Your Round in 20 Min

A ~30-minute conversation. Mostly logistics + fit, but at StubHub the recruiter sometimes goes deeper than expected — there are Blind reports of recruiters asking candidates to walk through a project in detail. Treat this as a mini hiring-manager screen, not just paperwork.

🚨 Critical Warning (from a 2025 Blind post)

One candidate reported being asked to walk through a project they fully owned for 15+ minutes on the recruiter call — and was rejected because the answer wasn't crisp. Have ONE project ready to talk through in 2 min, 5 min, and 15 min versions. Lead with the business problem, then the technical decision, then your specific contribution and the measurable result.

📋 What They'll Almost Certainly Ask
⭐ Opening Question
"Tell me about yourself and what drew you to StubHub."
💡 Keep it tight (90 seconds): current role → relevant scope (distributed systems / marketplace / scale) → why StubHub specifically. Mention you applied to both Supply Integrations and Supply Core — frame it as genuine interest in the Supply org's mission of automating seller inventory at scale, not "I'll take whatever."
🎯 Why StubHub / Why This Team
"Why are you interested in the Supply team specifically?"
💡 Strong answer hooks: (1) Two-sided marketplaces are hard — supply-side problems compound with demand-side spikes. (2) The Supply team's stated mission is "simplify life for large-ticket sellers via automation and APIs" — that's classic platform engineering work. (3) StubHub operates at real scale: 300M+ tickets, 195 countries, peak event spikes. Reference the actual JD language back at them.
📖 Project Walkthrough High likelihood
"Walk me through a project you fully owned end-to-end."
💡 Pick a project that mirrors Supply work: distributed system, API integration, high-throughput data, or seller/partner-facing tooling. Structure: Problem (with business stakes) → Your specific role → Key technical decision + tradeoff → Result (numbers if possible). Avoid "we did X" — say "I decided X."
🛠️ Tech Stack Alignment
"What's your experience with Java / C# / distributed systems?"
💡 Both JDs explicitly call out Java and C# as primary languages. For Supply Core also: React 2+ years. Be honest about depth in each — they're trying to confirm you can ramp without 3 months of language learning. If you're stronger in one, say so and explain the related transferable experience.
📍 Logistics
"Are you ok with hybrid 3 days/week in Seattle (or Aliso Viejo / NY for Supply Core)?"
💡 You're in Spokane — Seattle hybrid means relocation or a tough weekly commute. Be ready with a clear answer. If you're willing to relocate, say so clearly with a timeline. If you'd negotiate, frame as "let's discuss what flexibility looks like" rather than pushing back hard on the first call.
💰 Compensation Expectations
"What are you looking for compensation-wise?"
💡 Published band is $200K–$250K base + equity + 401k + benefits. Safe answer: "I'm looking for total comp in line with senior level at companies of StubHub's scale — base in your published range plus equity sounds reasonable, but I'd like to learn more about the role before committing to a number." Don't anchor low.
🕒 Timeline & Other Process
"Are you interviewing elsewhere? What's your timeline?"
💡 Honesty + light urgency works best. "I'm in process with [vague: a few other companies], and I'm looking to make a decision in the next 4–6 weeks. StubHub is high on my list because [specific Supply reason]." Creates polite pressure without sounding desperate.

🔑 Do's and Don'ts for the Next 20 Minutes

  • Do: Have a glass of water, your resume, the two JDs, and a notes page open
  • Do: Smile when you start — your voice changes. Recruiters notice energy
  • Do: Use the recruiter's name once or twice; mirror their pace
  • Do: Ask "What does your timeline look like for this role?" before you end the call
  • Don't: Volunteer salary expectations first — let them name the band, then react
  • Don't: Trash-talk current/past employers — even subtly. Recruiters relay this
  • Don't: Say "I'm flexible" on location without thinking — be specific
🙋 5 Questions to Ask the Recruiter

Recruiters love thoughtful questions — they signal you're serious and they need this info to sell you to the team.

Question 1 · The Role Itself
"Between Supply Integrations and Supply Core (Fullstack), which one has more urgency right now, and what would the deciding factor be in steering me toward one?"
💡 Shows you're thinking strategically about fit, not just collecting any offer. Also surfaces useful intel about team needs.
Question 2 · The Team
"Can you tell me about the hiring manager and the team I'd be joining — what kind of engineer thrives there?"
💡 Standard but valuable. Recruiter's answer tells you what to emphasize in the HM round.
Question 3 · Process Specifics
"What does the rest of the interview loop look like, and is there anything specific I should prepare for the coding or system design rounds?"
💡 Recruiters often give away the system design topic or coding format if you ask directly. Worth trying.
Question 4 · Hybrid Reality
"How strict is the 3-days-in-office policy in practice, and is there flexibility on the location split between Seattle / Aliso Viejo / NY for the Supply Core role?"
💡 Critical for you given Spokane. Better to surface this Day 1 than after onsite.
Question 5 · Timeline & Next Steps
"What's your target timeline to fill these roles, and when would I hear back about next steps?"
💡 Sets a clear expectation. StubHub recruiters have a reputation for slow follow-ups — anchor a date now so you can politely check in later.
💬 Behavioral / Director Round

The final round at StubHub is typically with a Director and is described in 2026 candidate writeups as "intense" — frequent interruptions, alternative-solution challenges, business-strategy questions, and even "propose a new idea and explain how it'd make money." This is NOT a soft round.

📐 Use STAR — But With a Business Layer at the Top

S
SITUATION
Set the scene briefly — including the business stakes, not just technical context.
T
TASK
What were YOU specifically responsible for? What was the desired outcome?
A
ACTION
Decisions YOU made — including tradeoffs and alternatives you rejected and why.
R
RESULT
Measurable outcome — $ saved, latency cut, users impacted. Numbers matter.
📋 Example Questions (from LinkJob 2026 + Glassdoor)
📖 Project Deep Dive (Almost Guaranteed)
"Walk me through one of your projects. What problem were you solving and why did the business care?"
💡 Be ready for interruptions. Don't get flustered when they cut in. Expect "Why didn't you do X?" or "Did you consider caching?" — sometimes the suggestion won't fit your context, but answer it seriously anyway, then defend your choice.
⚡ Conflict / Disagreement
"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate or manager on a technical approach."
💡 Show structured disagreement: understood their view, presented data, found common ground or escalated cleanly. Avoid "I was right and they were wrong" framing.
💼 Business Impact
"What business value did your work bring? How would you have monetized or scaled it further?"
💡 This is the StubHub-defining question. Have one project where you can tie engineering decisions to revenue, retention, or cost saved. Practice the "if we'd had more time, here's how I'd extend it" angle.
🌱 Failure & Learning
"Tell me about a time something you built failed or didn't deliver expected results."
💡 Pick a real failure with a real lesson. Show ownership, not blame. End with what you'd do differently and what you've applied since.
💡 Open-Ended Idea
"Pitch me a new idea — something StubHub could build — and explain how you'd make it successful."
💡 Reported by 2026 candidate. Prep ONE pitch in advance: a Supply-side idea (e.g., "AI-assisted pricing for individual sellers," or "API for ticket brokers to sync inventory from N primary sources at once"). Frame as: problem → user → solution → why now → success metric.
🎯 How to Prepare

🔑 StubHub-Specific Preparation Tips

  • Build a story bank of 5–6 STAR stories: ownership, conflict, failure, scale, mentorship, ambiguity. Each adaptable to multiple questions.
  • Tag every story with business impact: $ saved, users affected, latency, retention. If you can't quantify it, the story is weaker for StubHub.
  • Practice surviving interruptions: If interrupted, pause, answer the new question fully, then ask "Want me to go back to where I was?" Don't get rattled.
  • Top-down explanations always: Start with the problem & the why, then zoom into architecture, then code-level details — never reverse.
  • Have ONE pitch ready for "give me a new idea." Don't improvise this — too high-stakes.
  • Know StubHub's business model cold: they take a service + buyer fee on each sale. Supply-side fraud, broker integrations, and primary partner relationships are core.
✅Do
  • Open every story with the business problem, not the tech
  • Say "I" not "we" — they're hiring you, not your team
  • Quantify outcomes whenever possible
  • Stay calm under interruption; assume good intent
  • Reference StubHub's actual challenges (data migration, scale spikes, broker integrations)
❌Avoid
  • Architecture details before the "why this matters" frame
  • Vague answers ("a challenging project at my last job...")
  • Defensive responses to alternative-solution questions
  • Saying "I don't know" without a follow-up of "but here's how I'd find out"
  • Pure technical pride without commercial framing
🙋 5 Questions to Ask — Behavioral Round

In the Director round, smart questions signal seniority. Use these to probe what success looks like and where the real challenges sit.

Question 1 · Defining Success
"What does success look like for a Senior Engineer on the Supply team at the 6-month and 12-month mark — and what's the biggest indicator that someone is going to struggle?"
💡 The "struggle" half of the question is the real prize — Directors will tell you the actual failure modes, which tells you exactly what the team values.
Question 2 · The Real Challenges
"You mentioned [X] earlier — what are the biggest unsolved problems on the Supply side right now where a senior engineer could actually move the needle?"
💡 Forces the Director to describe real work, not platitudes. Their answer becomes ammunition for your follow-up conversations and offer negotiation.
Question 3 · Tech & Architecture Direction
"From a Director's view, where is the Supply platform going architecturally over the next 18 months? Any major migrations, modernizations, or rebuilds being considered?"
💡 Signals you think in years not sprints. Also useful intel for the system design round if you have it later.
Question 4 · Cross-Org Influence
"How does the Supply team work with the Consumer side, Data, and Trust & Safety? Are there well-defined interfaces or is it more ad-hoc?"
💡 Surfaces real organizational dynamics. Cross-functional pain is where senior engineers earn their keep.
Question 5 · Leadership & Growth
"How do you grow Senior engineers toward Staff at StubHub — is the path more technical depth, broader influence, or both? What's the most recent example?"
💡 Subtle signal that you're thinking about progression. If they can't name a recent Staff promo from their org, that's data too.
💻 Coding Rounds (OA + Pair Coding)

StubHub has TWO coding stages: a Codility online assessment early, and a 60–90 min pair coding round later. They test different things. The OA is correctness-focused LeetCode-medium; the pair coding is about building a small system iteratively with clean OOD.

📋 Stage A: Codility OA

  • Format: 1 question, 60–70 minutes, multiple test runs allowed before submission
  • Difficulty: LeetCode medium — arrays, hashmaps, sorting, string manipulation
  • What matters: correctness + edge cases. Not speed, not cleverness.
  • Hot tip from 2026 candidate: "The problem is often very close to a real LeetCode question." Run through Codility's medium-tier sample problems.

📋 Stage B: Pair Coding (Live, 60–90 min)

  • Style: Build a small system incrementally — interviewer adds requirements one at a time
  • Format: Collaborative, conversational. Looks more like work than LeetCode.
  • Focus: OOD, clean code, extensibility patterns (Strategy, Factory), not algorithm tricks
  • Real 2026 example: Build a Notification Engine — given Events (city, time) and Customers (city, birthday), select relevant events. Then add: same-city filter → birthday proximity → top-K nearest by coordinates → cheapest via external API → combine multiple filters extensibly
📋 Real Question Patterns (from LinkJob 2026 + Glassdoor)
🔔 Notification / Recommendation Engine Pair Coding (Recent)
Given Event(city, time, price) and Customer(city, birthday, coords), build a system that filters and ranks relevant events. Incremental follow-ups add: top-K nearest, cheapest via API, combining filters.
💡 Winning approach (from a successful 2026 candidate): start simple, then refactor as follow-ups arrive. Introduce a Filter interface (Strategy pattern) when "combine multiple filters" comes up. Swap sort for heap when "top-K" appears. Talk through your refactors out loud.
🔤 String / Data Manipulation
Parse, split, transform structured strings — e.g., ticket IDs, event tags, seat sections. Classic data-cleaning style.
💡 Multiple Glassdoor reports mention string manipulation in OAs. Standard: split/strip/join, char counting, anagram-style transforms, parsing structured input.
🏗️ Small Service Design + Implement
From Feb 2026 Glassdoor: "90 minute codepair where you design and implement something that represents a small service."
💡 Halfway between system design and coding. Spend the first 10 minutes on data model + interfaces. Don't just start typing.
📊 Top-K / Streaming
Older Glassdoor: "Given a stream of incoming tweets and a machine that can only store one tweet at a time, write a function that..." — reservoir sampling / streaming-stats family.
💡 Less common now but worth knowing. Reservoir sampling, running median, top-K heaps — these come up because they map to real StubHub streaming workloads (price updates, inventory changes).
🔄 Refactoring Asks
"Now refactor that for readability / extensibility / performance." Sometimes asked 2–3 times.
💡 Don't take it personally. They want to see how you respond to feedback. Common refactors: extract interface → strategy pattern → swap data structure for the access pattern.

🔑 Preparation Tips (Pair Coding Specifically)

  • Start simple, refactor when asked: Don't over-engineer Round 1. Build the dumb working version, then upgrade as follow-ups arrive.
  • Know your OOD patterns cold: Strategy, Factory, Observer, Decorator. Names matter — they convey seniority.
  • Talk while you code: Narrate decisions, especially tradeoffs. Silent coding kills your signal.
  • Ask before you assume: "Should I assume coords are always populated?" "What's the failure mode if the external API times out?" These questions are evaluated positively.
  • Language: Java or C# best matches the role; Python is acceptable but signals less alignment with the stack.
  • Test as you go: Walk through 1–2 inputs by hand before saying "done." Bug-catching mid-flight reads as senior.
🙋 5 Questions to Ask — Coding Round

The pair coding interviewer is likely a future peer — a senior engineer on the Supply team. Use these to learn what daily engineering life actually looks like.

Question 1 · Code Quality Bar
"What's the team's expectation around code review, test coverage, and documentation? How does that work in practice on Supply?"
💡 Tells you whether the team values craft or moves fast-and-loose. As a Senior engineer you'll be enforcing this standard.
Question 2 · The Languages in Practice
"The JD mentions Java and C# — what's the actual split on the Supply team? Are there services in one and not the other, or is it mixed?"
💡 Practical signal. If they say "we're consolidating onto X" that's a strategic data point about where the team's heading.
Question 3 · Dev Workflow
"What's the local dev experience like — can I run the Supply services on my laptop, or is there a heavy cloud-dev environment? How fast is the CI/CD loop?"
💡 Dev experience is a real productivity tax. If they can't answer cleanly, that tells you the DX has gaps — which could be your first impact area.
Question 4 · On-Call
"What does on-call look like for the Supply team? How often does it fire, and what kinds of issues come up most?"
💡 On-call quality is a leading indicator of system health. Frequent fires = tech debt or operational gaps. Quiet on-call = mature system. Both are useful to know.
Question 5 · Who You'd Work With
"Who would I be pairing with most in this role? What kinds of engineers are on the team right now?"
💡 Future-colleague visibility. Their answer reveals team composition, seniority mix, and whether you'd be the most senior IC or one of many.
🏗️ System Design Round

A standalone 60-minute round. 2026 candidates report a clear pattern: design a system tied to StubHub's real business — typically notifications, recommendations, or inventory management. Glassdoor Feb 2026 candidate warned the prompt can be over-scoped — the interviewer may dump 3–4 product requirements and expect you to drive scope yourself.

📋 Format (from Glassdoor Feb 2026 + LinkJob)

  • Duration: 60 minutes
  • Theme: Notifications, recommendations, or marketplace inventory at scale
  • Setup: Interviewer reads/pastes product requirements — sometimes 3–4 of them. You must clarify scope, what's already built vs. what you're designing.
  • Drives: Failure handling, retries, scale, dependency outage scenarios
  • Style: Guided but candidate-driven. They'll hint occasionally, but you own the design

⚠️ Scope-Management Warning (Feb 2026 Glassdoor)

One 2026 candidate explicitly called out: the interviewer "copied and pasted in four product requirements and read them word for word" — and the candidate then struggled because they weren't sure which parts were existing services they could call vs. things they needed to design. Spend your first 5–8 minutes aggressively clarifying scope. Ask: "Is X an existing service I can assume?" "Are we designing the data pipeline, the API, or both?" Don't start architecting until scope is locked.

📋 Example Questions (from 2026 candidate reports)
📧 Event Recommendation Notification System Most Common 2026
Design a system that filters events and sends relevant email/push notifications to users at scale. Include: filtering pipeline, batch vs. real-time, retry/failure handling, monitoring.
💡 Building blocks: User profile service → Event catalog → Matching/scoring engine → Notification dispatcher (Kafka queue) → Channel adapters (email/SMS/push) → Delivery tracking + retries. Discuss DLQ, idempotency, and what happens when the email provider is down.
🎫 Ticket Inventory / Listing System Supply-Relevant
Design the system that lets sellers upload, update, and manage ticket inventory across millions of listings — with consistency guarantees so no ticket is double-sold.
💡 Highly likely given your Supply role. Cover: write path (validation, fraud check, ledger), read path (search, filters, caching), concurrency control to prevent double-sells (optimistic locking, distributed lock, or atomic state-machine transitions), event-sourcing for audit trail.
🔌 Partner Integration / Supply API
Design an API that lets large ticket brokers sync inventory from their inventory management systems (IMS) into StubHub — bidirectionally, in near-real-time, across 100+ partners.
💡 Especially relevant for Supply Integrations. Cover: pull vs. push (webhooks + polling fallback), partner authentication, rate limits per partner, schema mapping, partner-specific failure isolation, replayable events.
📈 Holiday Spike Scalability
How would you scale StubHub for a 10x spike during a Super Bowl / Taylor Swift tour drop?
💡 Standard scalability playbook: CDN for read traffic, horizontal scale on app tier, read replicas + caching (Redis) on DB tier, async job queues for non-critical writes, circuit breakers and graceful degradation, virtual waiting room for checkout.
🗄️ Database Schema for Booking System
Design the schema for users, sellers, listings, transactions, payments, refunds. Show relationships, indexes, how you'd handle concurrent purchases.
💡 SQL preferred. Don't forget: soft deletes for audit, event log table for state transitions, indexes on hot query paths (event_id + status), and explicit unique constraints to prevent double-allocation.

🔑 Preparation Tips

  • Think marketplace, not generic CRUD: StubHub is two-sided. Always ask "who's the user — buyer or seller?" and design for both.
  • Lock scope before architecting: Spend 5–8 min on clarifying questions. This isn't optional given the Feb 2026 warning.
  • Sketch always: Boxes-and-arrows on a shared whiteboard (Excalidraw, the platform's built-in tool, or paper held up to camera).
  • Drive the conversation toward failure modes: Retries, idempotency, DLQ, partial failures. StubHub specifically asks about "what if dependencies go down" — be ready.
  • Numbers matter: Estimate QPS, storage, payload size early. "Let's assume 1M users × 100 events/day = 100M emails/day → ~1200 QPS peak" anchors the rest of the design.
  • Reference real StubHub challenges: data migration (mentioned by interviewers as current pain), Black Friday-style spikes, fraud protection — bonus points for awareness.

"Where is StubHub's Supply platform today on the monolith-to-microservices spectrum, and what's the strategic direction over the next year or two?"
💡 Frames you as someone thinking about architecture trajectory. Their answer is also useful intel for negotiating scope of your future work.

"What's the hardest unsolved system design problem on the Supply side right now? Where would a new senior engineer have the most leverage?"
💡 Direct, specific, and self-serving in the best way — their answer tells you exactly what to emphasize in your own background.

"One candidate mentioned data migration as a current Supply challenge — is that still active? What does it look like at this point?"
💡 Shows you did real research from candidate reports. Migration work is classic senior-engineer territory and they'll perk up if you've thought about it.
Question 4 · Cross-Team Dependencies
"How tightly does the Supply platform interact with Trust & Safety, Payments, and the Consumer side? Are those well-defined service boundaries or more interleaved?"
💡 Signals you understand a marketplace is a federation of systems, not a monolith. Surfaces real organizational dynamics.
Question 5 · International / viagogo
"StubHub serves 195 countries with viagogo. From a Supply perspective, how much of the codebase is shared across markets vs. region-specific — and is that something a senior engineer would touch?"
💡 Very few candidates will ask this. International + regulatory + multi-currency is real complexity, and asking signals you think about platform scope, not just features.

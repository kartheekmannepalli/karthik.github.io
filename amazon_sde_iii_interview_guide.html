🗺️ Full Interview Process at Amazon (SDE III)

Based on Amazon's own SDE III/Sr. SDE prep page plus dozens of Glassdoor, Onsites.fyi, LeetCode and Blind candidate reports. The defining feature of an Amazon loop is that every round blends technical signal with Leadership Principles — and a Bar Raiser sits in the loop with veto power.

1
Application / Recruiter Screen
Resume fit + a deep dive into leadership and impact experience.
2
Technical Phone Screen
60 min with a senior leader. ~½ Leadership Principles, ~½ coding + system design.
3
Screen Outcome
If successful, a recruiter arranges your interview loop.
4
Interview Loop (5 rounds)
Five 55-min interviews: coding, system design, behavioral — each scored on assigned LPs.
5
Bar Raiser
One trained interviewer (outside the team) safeguards the hiring bar.
6
Decision
Debrief + outcome typically within 5 business days.
📦About Amazon

Amazon is one of the world's largest technology companies, spanning the e-commerce marketplace, AWS (its highly profitable cloud arm), devices like Alexa and Kindle, a fast-growing advertising business, and global logistics. Engineering centers on massive scale, low latency, and reliability — Prime Day-style traffic spikes, multi-region availability, and tight cost control are everyday realities. SDE III engineers typically own cross-service or platform-level systems.

🎯What an SDE III Does
  • Plays a technical leadership role; the team turns to them for technical and business guidance
  • Builds multiple high-performance, stable, scalable systems
  • Holds a system-wide, architectural view of solutions
  • Writes exemplary, secure, maintainable, extensible code others can build on
  • Mentors SDE I/II engineers and sets team-wide best practices
🛡️The Bar Raiser (Amazon-specific)
  • A specially trained interviewer from outside the hiring team
  • Their job is to protect Amazon's long-term hiring bar, not to fill the seat
  • Has effective veto power in the debrief
  • Probes Leadership Principles hard and looks for consistent signal
  • Treat every interviewer as potentially the Bar Raiser — you won't be told who it is

📌 How decisions are made

Each interviewer is assigned specific competencies and Leadership Principles to evaluate, writes up detailed notes independently, and then the panel debriefs together. Consistent, well-evidenced signal across interviewers carries the most weight — one strong round rarely offsets a weak or vague one. Amazon is explicitly data-driven, so quantified results in your stories matter.

📈 SDE II → SDE III: What Changes

SDE III (L6) is not just a more productive SDE II. The jump is from owning a service well to shaping technical direction across services and influencing leaders. Understanding this reframes how you tell every story and approach every design.

⚡ The Core Shift in One Sentence

SDE II: Delivers scalable components and owns a service end-to-end within the team.
SDE III: Drives cross-service / platform-level architecture, aligns Directors and PMs, mentors senior engineers, and ties technical decisions to business outcomes (cost, revenue, reliability at org scale). You're judged on leverage and judgment, not just throughput.

🧠 Mindset Comparison

🔵 SDE II Thinking

  • "How do I build this service correctly and scalably?"
  • "How do I hit my latency and error-rate targets?"
  • "What's the best technical approach for this problem?"
  • "How do I mentor the juniors on my team?"
  • "I need to ship this feature on time with quality."

🟣 SDE III Thinking

  • "Should we build this at all? Does it serve the multi-year roadmap?"
  • "How does one decision unblock several teams or cut cost org-wide?"
  • "What standard or mechanism stops this class of failure for good?"
  • "How do I grow an SDE II into a strong senior engineer?"
  • "What's the technical strategy that aligns with the P&L?"
🎯 What to Keep in Mind During Your SDE III Loop
🔑 Lead with Impact, Then Implementation
Open each story with the business or org-level outcome before the technical detail. Amazon expects quantified results ("cut infra cost by $2M/yr", "enabled $50M incremental revenue on Prime Day").
💡 A common SDE III mistake: diving straight into low-level technical detail. Zoom out to scope, stakeholders, and impact first — then go deep when asked (Dive Deep rewards both).
✖️ Show Yourself as a Multiplier
For each story, answer: "How did this make other teams or engineers faster, safer, or more independent?" Mechanisms, runbooks, and standards count as much as the system you shipped.
💡 Maps directly to Hire and Develop the Best and Insist on the Highest Standards.
🌐 Demonstrate Cross-Org Influence
Bring examples of aligning engineering with product, data science, and Director/VP-level stakeholders — without formal authority.
💡 Maps to Earn Trust and Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit. Show you challenged respectfully, then committed fully.
🗺️ Speak to Technical Strategy, Not Just Execution
In design rounds, justify why an architecture matters for Amazon's multi-year scale and cost, and name the trade-offs at the org level (consistency vs availability, build vs buy).
💡 Maps to Think Big and Invent and Simplify. Think in years and multi-region, not single sprints.
🌫️ Prove You Can Navigate Ambiguity
Have at least one story where there was no clear spec or owner, and you created structure and drove it to resolution across stakeholders.
💡 Maps to Bias for Action and Ownership. If your stories always begin with "we were given a clear requirement," they read as SDE II.

📋 Preparation Checklist — SDE III Level

  • Reframe your stories: Upgrade SDE II-level STAR stories — add the business-impact layer, cross-team influence, and org-level outcome.
  • Prepare 4–6 flexible "defining project" stories that each flex across several Leadership Principles, rather than one thin story per principle.
  • Know your "multiplier" moments: times you made other engineers or teams meaningfully faster, safer, or more independent.
  • Be ready for "technical vision": how you'd shape the direction of a system or platform area.
  • Don't hide behind "we": the panel probes for your specific decisions and leadership — own them clearly.
  • Quantify everything: Amazon is data-driven; have real numbers (latency, RPS, cost, revenue, uptime) ready.

Designing Data-Intensive Applications (Martin Kleppmann) is the go-to for the distributed-systems depth expected at SDE III — especially the chapters on replication, partitioning, transactions, and consistency. For the leadership framing, The Staff Engineer's Path (Tanya Reilly) helps you articulate cross-org impact.

🎯 Leadership Principles & Behavioral Core of Every Round

Amazon weaves its 16 Leadership Principles (LPs) into every interview — not just one behavioral round. Each interviewer asks 2–3 behavioral questions tied to assigned LPs, on the premise that past behavior predicts future success. No brain teasers; they focus on the what, the how, and the why of your decisions, backed by data.

📐 Use the STAR Method — Amazon explicitly expects it

S
SITUATION
Set the scene briefly — context, scale, what was at stake.
T
TASK
Your specific responsibility or the decision you owned.
A
ACTION
What YOU did — the bulk of the answer. "I", not "we".
R
RESULT
Quantified outcome + what you learned. Numbers always.
🧭 The 16 Leadership Principles (paraphrased)

Customer Obsession

Start from the customer and work backwards; earn and keep their trust.

Ownership

Think long-term, act for the whole company; never say "not my job."

Invent and Simplify

Expect innovation; relentlessly find ways to simplify.

Are Right, A Lot

Strong judgment; seek diverse views and disconfirm your own beliefs.

Learn and Be Curious

Never done learning; explore new possibilities.

Hire and Develop the Best

Raise the bar with each hire; coach and grow others.

Insist on the Highest Standards

Relentlessly high bar; don't let defects pass down the line.

Think Big

Create a bold direction; look around corners for customers.

Bias for Action

Speed matters; many decisions are reversible — take calculated risks.

Frugality

Do more with less; constraints breed resourcefulness.

Earn Trust

Listen, speak candidly, be self-critical, treat others with respect.

Dive Deep

Stay connected to details; audit often; question gaps between metrics and anecdotes.

Have Backbone; Disagree & Commit

Challenge respectfully, then commit fully once decided.

Deliver Results

Focus on key inputs; deliver with quality, on time, despite setbacks.

Strive to be Earth's Best Employer

Build a safer, higher-performing, more empowering workplace.

Success & Scale Bring Broad Responsibility

Be humble about impact; leave things better than you found them.

📋 Example Questions (mapped to LPs — from Glassdoor, Onsites.fyi & candidate reports)
🛒 Customer Obsession / Deliver Results
Tell me about a time you made a decision that prioritized the customer even though it was harder or costlier for your team. What was the measurable outcome?
💡 Lead with the customer pain and the data; close with the quantified result and what changed long-term.
🤝 Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit
Describe a time you disagreed with a senior leader or another team on a technical direction. How did you handle it, and what happened after the decision was made?
💡 Show respectful challenge backed by evidence, then full commitment once the call was made — not lingering resentment.
🔍 Dive Deep / Ownership
Walk me through a critical production incident or outage you owned. How did you root-cause it, and what mechanism did you put in place so it stayed fixed?
💡 SDE III signal: not just the fix, but the systemic prevention (alarms, runbooks, design change) that raised the bar org-wide.
💡 Think Big / Invent and Simplify
Tell me about a long-term technical vision or migration you drove (e.g., replacing a legacy system). How did you align stakeholders and sequence the work?
💡 Multi-year framing wins here — e.g., a phased migration with measurable milestones and a clear simplification payoff.
🌱 Hire and Develop the Best / Earn Trust
Tell me about a time you mentored an engineer or raised the team's bar. How did you measure that you actually made them (or the team) better?
💡 Quantify the multiplier: promotions, reduced review cycles, fewer incidents, onboarding time cut.
🎯 How to Prepare

🔑 Amazon-Specific Preparation Tips

  • Build a story bank mapped to LPs: 4–6 strong projects, each tagged to the 3–4 LPs it can demonstrate. One rich story can cover Ownership + Deliver Results + Dive Deep.
  • Quantify relentlessly: Amazon is data-driven. Every story needs metrics — RPS, latency, cost, uptime, revenue, headcount saved.
  • Prepare failure stories too: they probe risks taken, failures, and what you learned. A polished-only narrative reads as untrustworthy (Earn Trust = be vocally self-critical).
  • Use "I", not "we": interviewers must isolate your contribution. Name your specific decisions.
  • Have follow-up depth ready: expect "why did you choose that?", "what would you do differently?", "what was the data?" — Dive Deep is constant.
  • Connect to the mission: tie your engineering philosophy to customer obsession and operating at scale.

📌 Where LPs show up in the loop

The technical phone screen spends roughly half its time on LPs. In the onsite loop, behavioral questions appear in every round (typically 2–3 per interviewer), including inside the coding and design rounds. The Bar Raiser in particular pressure-tests LP signal. Treat the behavioral bar as equally decisive as the technical bar — community reports consistently note strong coders get rejected on weak LP answers.

✅Do
  • Be specific — real numbers, systems, and outcomes
  • Show ownership ("I decided…", "I owned…")
  • Include a genuine failure and the lesson learned
  • Map each story to the LP being probed
  • Pause to structure your answer in STAR order
❌Avoid
  • Vague answers ("I worked on a challenging project…")
  • Hiding behind "we" — your contribution disappears
  • Stories with no metrics or business impact
  • Harshly criticizing past employers or teammates
  • Only success stories — no risk, no growth shown
🙋 5 Questions to Ask — Behavioral / Leadership Round

You're evaluating Amazon too. These signal SDE III-level thinking about scope, ownership, and how LPs actually operate day-to-day.

Question 1 · Scope & Impact
"What does success look like for an SDE III on this team at the 6-month and 12-month mark, and how is impact measured beyond shipped code?"
💡 Surfaces how the team defines L6 value and lets you calibrate whether expectations match your strengths.
Question 2 · Leadership Principles in Practice
"Which Leadership Principles does this team lean on most when there's tension — say, Bias for Action vs. Insist on the Highest Standards — and how do you resolve those trade-offs?"
💡 Shows you understand LPs are operating tools, not slogans, and that real decisions require balancing them.
Question 3 · Ownership & Autonomy
"How much autonomy does an SDE III have to identify and start high-impact projects versus being assigned them? Can you give a recent example?"
💡 Probes whether the role is genuinely self-directing — central to operating at L6.
Question 4 · Cross-Org Influence
"How do senior engineers here drive alignment with product and Director-level stakeholders — through documents, reviews, or something else?"
💡 Amazon runs on written narratives; the answer tells you how influence actually flows.
Question 5 · Growth & the Bar
"How does the team raise its engineering bar over time, and what support exists for an SDE III who wants to grow toward Principal?"
💡 Aligns with Hire and Develop the Best and shows you think about the long arc, not just the next role.
💻 Coding Round

Amazon expects syntactically correct, runnable code — no pseudocode. The stated evaluation criteria are efficiency, reliability, robustness, portability, maintainability, and readability. At SDE III, problems lean toward optimization for scale, concurrency, and production-grade quality, and interviewers add follow-ups and edge cases as you go. Expect 2–3 coding rounds in the loop, each also carrying Leadership Principle questions.

📋 Format (from Amazon's prep page, Glassdoor & candidate reports)

  • Style: Live coding in a shared editor (Amazon's "Livecode" / Chime) or whiteboard onsite — narrate your thinking throughout.
  • Difficulty: LeetCode medium, occasionally hard; SDE III sees more follow-ups around concurrency, scale, and trade-offs.
  • Language: Your choice — Java and Python are most common.
  • Duration: 55 minutes per round; coding usually ~35–40 min after LP questions.
  • Non-negotiables: handle edge cases, validate bad input, state time/space complexity, and write tests.
📋 Example Questions (from Onsites.fyi, LeetCode Discuss & Glassdoor)
🗃️ Concurrency + Caching (very common at SDE III)
Implement an LRU cache, then extend it: make it thread-safe, add TTL-based eviction, and discuss scaling to thousands of concurrent updates.
💡 Reads aren't passive — every get() updates recency. Start with a clean HashMap + doubly-linked-list version, then layer locking/TTL as the follow-ups arrive.
⏱️ Rate Limiting / Scheduling at Scale
Design a job scheduler that handles ~1M tasks/sec with priority queues, or a distributed semaphore / rate limiter coordinating across many nodes.
💡 Discuss data-structure choice (heaps), back-pressure, and how coordination would work across machines (token bucket, centralized vs. local counters).
🌐 Graphs & Trees (bread-and-butter patterns)
Number of Islands, Course Schedule (cycle/topological sort), right-side view of a binary tree, or word-ladder / shortest-transformation BFS.
💡 Amazon questions cluster on a small set of patterns — BFS/DFS, topological sort, sliding window, heaps. Recognize the pattern fast and state it out loud.
🔢 Heaps / Top-K & Streaming
K Closest Points to Origin, top-K frequent elements, or "for each element return the nearest element to its right with strictly higher frequency."
💡 Heap vs. quickselect trade-offs are a great place to show complexity reasoning. Mention streaming variants for the scale follow-up.
🏗️ Production-grade Data Structure / OOP
Design a thread-safe, memory-efficient in-memory store with snapshot isolation, or model a system (parking lot, deck of cards) with clean classes and methods.
💡 SDE III bar: clean interfaces, testability, extensibility, and explicit handling of concurrency and invalid input — not just a passing function.

🔑 Preparation Tips

  • Drill the high-frequency patterns: arrays/strings (two-pointer, sliding window), graphs (BFS/DFS, topological sort), trees, heaps/top-K, and linked lists. Filter LeetCode by the Amazon tag.
  • Practice clean structure under pressure: clarify constraints → state a one-sentence plan → code one clean function → run 2 quick tests including edge cases → state complexity → discuss optimizations.
  • Get comfortable with concurrency: locks, thread-safety, and at least one concurrency primitive in your language (Java CompletableFuture/ConcurrentHashMap, Go goroutines/channels).
  • Narrate constantly: Amazon evaluates your thought process, not just the final answer. Talk through trade-offs as you go.
  • Write tests proactively: "no bad input slips through" is an explicit criterion — validate inputs and cover edge cases without being asked.
🙋 5 Questions to Ask — Coding Round

Your interviewer is a future peer. These questions show you care about engineering craft and production quality at SDE III scale.

Question 1 · Engineering Standards
"Beyond passing tests, what defines high-quality production code on this team — code review norms, test-coverage expectations, and how those standards are enforced?"
💡 As an SDE III you'd help set these. Signals you think about the bar at a team level, not just this exercise.
Question 2 · Languages & Stack
"What's the primary language and stack for this team's services, and are there modernization efforts underway — newer frameworks, async patterns, or service decomposition?"
💡 SDE III engineers often own these migrations; shows you think about the codebase's trajectory.
Question 3 · Developer Experience
"How does the team handle developer productivity — local setup, CI/CD speed, build tooling? Is there room for SDE III-led DX improvements?"
💡 DX is a classic senior multiplier; signals your leverage extends beyond shipping features.
Question 4 · Operational Excellence
"How does the team handle on-call, incident response, and operational load? How does a senior engineer shape reliability — runbooks, COEs/post-mortems, SLOs?"
💡 Amazon prizes operational excellence; at peak (Prime Day) reliability is existential. Asking shows operational maturity.
Question 5 · Testing & Quality Culture
"How does the team balance Bias for Action with Insist on the Highest Standards when shipping — where do you draw the line on test coverage and tech debt?"
💡 Connects coding craft to the LPs and reveals how the team actually makes quality trade-offs.
🏗️ System Design Round

Amazon notes that designing systems is distinctive because of its size and speed of change. Expect at least one system design question (SDE III loops often have two, plus an architecture-review/critique round). The stated objectives are practicality, accuracy, efficiency, reliability, optimization, and scalability. The interviewer probes your design; you're expected to ask clarifying questions to complete and validate it.

📋 Format (from Amazon's prep page, Onsites.fyi & Glassdoor)

  • Covers high-level design (HLD): APIs, data flow, scalability, multi-region; and sometimes low-level design (LLD): class/object modeling — team-dependent.
  • Open-ended by design — there is no single right answer; reasoning and trade-offs matter most.
  • SDE III bar: multi-region, fault-tolerant, disaster-recovery-grade systems with explicit consistency and cost trade-offs.
  • An architecture-review round may ask you to critique and improve an existing system (find bottlenecks, cut cost, harden security).
  • AWS fluency is expected — DynamoDB, S3, Kinesis, Lambda, Aurora, Route 53, plus CAP-theorem reasoning.
📋 Example Questions (from Onsites.fyi, Glassdoor & candidate reports)
🔍 Recommendation / Personalization at Scale
Design Amazon's product recommendation engine for ~100M users with <50ms p95 latency and real-time personalization that survives an AZ/region outage.
💡 Cover clickstream ingestion (Kafka/Kinesis), model serving + A/B testing, caching (DAX), latency-based global routing, and the cold-start problem.
📈 Peak-Traffic Scalability
How would you design a service to absorb a 10x spike (Prime Day) without falling over?
💡 Horizontal scaling, load balancing, multi-layer caching (Redis/DAX), read replicas, async queues (SQS/Kinesis), CDN, and graceful degradation / circuit breakers.
🗄️ Distributed Data & Consistency
Design a globally consistent (or eventually consistent) key-value store, or a real-time fraud-detection system for Amazon Payments.
💡 This is where CAP, quorums, replication, and DynamoDB Global Tables / CRDTs earn their keep. Be explicit about consistency vs. availability choices and why.
🌊 Real-Time Streaming
Design an end-to-end real-time streaming application — ingestion via APIs, maintaining stream context/state, and downstream processing. (Reported on Glassdoor.)
💡 Talk through stateful stream processing, partitioning by key, exactly-once vs. at-least-once delivery, and backpressure handling.
🔧 Architecture Review / Critique
Review an existing system (e.g., the product catalog service) and propose optimizations across performance, cost, scalability, and security.
💡 SDE III signal: methodically find bottlenecks (missing indexes, hot partitions), quantify cost savings (tiered storage), and sequence improvements by impact.

🔑 Preparation Tips

  • Drive a repeatable framework: clarify requirements & constraints (QPS, latency, data size) → API contract → high-level diagram → data model → deep dive → failure modes → trade-offs. Ask questions throughout.
  • Quantify and estimate: back-of-envelope capacity math (traffic, storage, bandwidth) shows the practicality Amazon scores on.
  • Be fluent in AWS primitives: when to reach for DynamoDB vs. Aurora, S3 tiers, Kinesis vs. SQS, Lambda vs. ECS/Fargate — and the cost implications.
  • Always cover failure & recovery: region/AZ outage, retries, idempotency, circuit breakers, monitoring (CloudWatch/X-Ray). Reliability is an explicit objective.
  • Read DDIA: Designing Data-Intensive Applications chapters on replication, partitioning, transactions, and consistency map almost directly onto these rounds.
  • Sketch as you go: use a shared whiteboard/Excalidraw and keep the diagram readable; defend each box.
🙋 5 Questions to Ask — System Design Round

The design interviewer is likely a senior/principal engineer — your future peer. These show strategic depth and that you grasp the real complexity of operating at Amazon scale.

Question 1 · Architecture Direction
"Where is this team's architecture today on the monolith-to-microservices spectrum, and what's the strategic direction? Would an SDE III help drive those decisions?"
💡 Shows you see architectural evolution as a senior concern and scopes the real work you'd own.
Question 2 · Scale & Hardest Problems
"What are the hardest unsolved scalability or reliability challenges this team faces — the ones that keep you up before peak events?"
💡 Surfaces the genuine technical frontier and signals you think beyond generic patterns.
Question 3 · Data & Consistency Trade-offs
"How does the team reason about consistency vs. availability across regions, and which data stores (DynamoDB, Aurora, etc.) anchor the critical paths?"
💡 Demonstrates distributed-systems maturity and curiosity about the real constraints, not textbook answers.
Question 4 · Decision & Doc Process
"How are major architectural decisions made and documented here — design docs, narratives, architecture reviews? How much influence does an SDE III have in shaping those processes?"
💡 Amazon's written-narrative culture is distinctive; the answer reveals how technical influence actually flows.
Question 5 · Cost & Operational Trade-offs
"How does the team balance Frugality against reliability and speed when designing — for example, deciding between a managed AWS service and building in-house?"
💡 Connects design directly to the Leadership Principles and shows you weigh cost as a first-class design constraint, as Amazon does.
